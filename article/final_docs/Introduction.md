# From Review Text to Explainable Restaurant Recommendations
A four-part study of corpus construction, leakage-safe semantic features generation, architecture-dependent ranking and re-ranking, and incorporating a GraphRAG layer for explainability.
## Introduction
Restaurant recommendation is a ranking problem under incomplete information. A system must select a small set of potentially relevant restaurants from a large catalogue, often using only prior interactions and a few attributes such as cuisine, price, and location. These inputs can capture broad behavioral patterns, but they provide limited information about factors that may determine whether a restaurant suits a particular diner, including dietary accommodation, atmosphere, service pace, and occasion.
Review text offers a possible source for these missing signals. In the corpus developed for this study, declared price level is missing for 59.2% of restaurants and an aggregate platform rating for 43.6%, whereas text is present for 83.6% of review records. Reviews are therefore substantially denser than the structured metadata, although their content is heterogeneous, subjective, and not uniformly informative.
This study examines whether review text can be converted into useful recommendation features while preserving the temporal boundary required for valid evaluation. It addresses three questions. First, can a locally deployed language model derive structured attributes and semantic representations without using reviews associated with held-out interactions? Second, does adding these features improve ranking performance, and does their effect differ across recommender architectures? Third, after a restaurant has been ranked, can a retrieval-based explanation layer produce a rationale supported by inspectable evidence?
The study is based on 75,203 restaurants and 5,544,947 reviews from 3,930,034 identified reviewers. Collection targeted Census block groups with high population density or foot traffic rather than sampling the United States uniformly. The resulting corpus has broad geographic coverage, but may not be a representative sample of U.S. restaurants or diners.
The four parts follow the resulting pipeline from data construction to explanation.
### Part 1, “Building a Geographically Targeted U.S. Restaurant-Review Corpus,”:
Describes the collection strategy and establishes the limits of the available evidence. It examines geographic coverage, metadata completeness, rating concentration, reviewer activity, and demographic patterns inferred from reviewer's name. The analysis shows that ratings are strongly concentrated at five stars and that the users eligible for modeling represent a small, highly active subset of reviewers. Demographic comparisons are treated as descriptive diagnostics because they rely on probabilistic name inference rather than self-reported identity.
### Part 2, “Deriving Leakage Safe Semantic Features from Restaurant Reviews,”
Converts training period review text into structured attributes, dense representations, and dish related signals. Values are retained only when the source text provides direct evidence; an absent mention is recorded as unknown rather than interpreted as a negative observation. A source level audit found no overlap between the reviews used to construct the audited features and the held out reviews.
### Part 3, “Evaluating LLM-Derived Features, Graph Architectures, and Proximity Reranking,”:
Compares a feature aware Two-Tower model, LightGCN, and a Knowledge Graph Attention Network with Stability-Aware Learning (KGAT-SAL) under a fixed chronological protocol. The same feature bundle has different effects across architectures: it does not reliably change the Two-Tower baseline, improves LightGCN across the evaluated seeds, and produces the largest gain for KGAT-SAL, increasing Hit@10 by 11.8% and NDCG@10 by 12.3% relative to the corresponding conventional-feature condition. A separately tuned proximity reranker produces a substantially larger improvement. A rating aware diagnostic further shows that among the held out visits retrieved in the top 10, more than 78% were restaurants the diner rated four or five stars.
### Part 4, “Auditing Post-Ranking GraphRAG Explanations for Provenance and Grounding,”:
Evaluates an explanation layer applied only after ranking. This separation prevents the explanation system from altering the recommendation order and means that its output should be interpreted as a grounded post-hoc rationale, not as a trace of the ranker’s internal computation. In an audit of 100 recommendations, all citation identifiers resolve, no evidence bundle includes a held-out review, and short-quotation fidelity is 95%. Model-judged explanation quality is less consistent, particularly for personalized claims. Examination of the recorded failure reasons indicates that low scores combine generation errors with ambiguities in the evidence schema and evaluation rubric; they should therefore not be treated as a direct estimate of unsupported-generation frequency.

The results show that review derived features can improve ranking when the receiving architecture can leverage the added relational and semantic structure. The improvement from the sematic features are architecture-dependent and and even higher gains were attained by adding an explicit geographic prior. Post-ranking retrieval can provide strong evidence provenance, but reliable personalized explanations require clearer evidence attribution and further human evaluation. This methodology offers a practical framework for food-delivery and restaurant-discovery applications seeking to provide more personalized, evidence-grounded recommendations.
