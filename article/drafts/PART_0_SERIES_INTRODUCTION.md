# From Review Text to Explainable Restaurant Recommendations
A four-part study of corpus construction, leakage-safe semantic features generation, architecture-dependent ranking and re-ranking, and incorporating a GraphRAG layer for explainability.
## Introduction
Restaurant recommendation is a ranking problem under incomplete information. A system must select a small set of potentially relevant restaurants from a large catalogue, often using only prior interactions and a few attributes such as cuisine, price, and location. These inputs can capture broad behavioral patterns, but they provide limited information about factors that may determine whether a restaurant suits a particular diner, including dietary accommodation, atmosphere, service pace, and occasion. Review text offers a possible source for these missing signals. In the corpus developed for this study, declared price level is missing for 59% of restaurants and an aggregate platform rating for 44%, whereas text is present for 84% of review records. Reviews are therefore substantially denser than the structured metadata, although their content is heterogeneous, subjective, and not uniformly informative.
This study examines whether review text can be transformed into semantic and contextual recommendation features that are difficult to capture consistently with conventional task-specific NLP methods, while preserving the temporal separation required for valid evaluation. It addresses three questions. First, can review text be converted into structured attributes and semantic representations without using evidence from held-out interactions? Second, do these features improve ranking performance, and does their effect vary across recommender architectures? Third, after a restaurant has been ranked, can a retrieval-based explanation layer produce a rationale grounded in verifiable evidence?
Data collection targeted Census block groups with high population density or foot traffic, resulting in a corpus with broad geographic coverage. The four parts follow the recommendation pipeline in sequence: corpus construction, semantic feature engineering, graph-based ranking and proximity reranking, and post-ranking explanation.
### Part 1, “Building a Geographically Targeted U.S. Restaurant-Review Corpus”:
Describes the collection strategy and establishes the limits of the available evidence. It examines geographic coverage, metadata completeness, rating concentration, reviewer activity, and demographic patterns inferred from reviewer's name. The analysis shows that ratings are strongly concentrated at five stars and that the users eligible for modeling represent a small, highly active subset of reviewers. Demographic comparisons are treated as descriptive diagnostics because they rely on probabilistic name inference rather than self-reported identity.
### Part 2, “Deriving Leakage Safe Semantic Features from Restaurant Reviews,”
Converts training period review text into structured attributes, dense representations, and dish related signals. Values are retained only when the source text provides direct evidence; an absent mention is recorded as unknown rather than interpreted as a negative observation. A source level audit found no overlap between the reviews used to construct the audited features and the held out reviews.
### Part 3, “Evaluating LLM-Derived Features, Graph Architectures, and Proximity Reranking,”:
Compares a feature-aware Two-Tower model, LightGCN, and a Knowledge Graph Attention Network with Stability-Aware Learning (KGAT-SAL) under a fixed chronological protocol. The same feature bundle has different effects across architectures: it does not reliably change the Two-Tower baseline, improves LightGCN across the evaluated seeds, and produces the largest gain for KGAT-SAL, increasing Hit@10 by 11.8% and NDCG@10 by 12.3% relative to the corresponding conventional-feature condition. A separately tuned proximity reranker produces a substantially larger improvement. A rating aware diagnostic further shows that among the held out visits retrieved in the top 10, more than 78% were restaurants the diner rated four or five stars.
### Part 4, “Auditing Post-Ranking GraphRAG Explanations for Provenance and Grounding,”:
Evaluates the need for an explanation layer that makes recommendations inspectable and allows diners to assess the evidence, personalization, and potential caveats behind a ranked result. GraphRAG is well suited to this architecture because KGAT-SAL already represents diners, restaurants, attributes, and their relationships as a typed graph, allowing the explanation layer to retrieve a compact evidence subgraph anchored on the selected diner–restaurant pair. Applied after ranking, it cannot alter the recommendation order and produces a grounded post-hoc rationale rather than a trace of the ranker’s internal computation. In an audit of 100 recommendations, every citation resolves, no evidence bundle contains a held-out review identifier, and supported diner-to-restaurant matches are available and cited in 79 cases. Model-judged grounding is high, with one unsupported claim, and removing the match evidence reduces usefulness and personalization without materially changing grounding.
## Conclusion
The results show that the value of review-derived semantic features depends on the recommender architecture: the features improved LightGCN and KGAT-SAL but did not reliably improve the Two-Tower model. Proximity reranking produced a larger gain, demonstrating the importance of explicitly incorporating geographic context. The post-ranking GraphRAG layer provided traceable, held-out-safe evidence, and explicit diner-to-restaurant matches improved the usefulness and personalization of its rationales without reducing model-judged grounding. Broader sampling and independent human evaluation remain necessary. Together, these findings provide a practical basis for developing more personalized, evidence-grounded recommendations in restaurant-discovery and food-delivery applications.
